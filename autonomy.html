Root cause 5 of 5

"I have no choice, and no way to notice my own focus."

Solution: build choice and self-monitoring. Students who can't sense their own focus state can't manage it. These warm-ups hand back some control and build the skill of noticing.

Same underlying skills as the other four folders — but here, the choice itself is part of the point.

Focus Meter Self-Check

At the start of the warm-up, students privately mark where they are on a 1–5 focus scale. No consequence attached — just practice noticing, built up over weeks.

Choose-Your-Warm-Up Menu

Offer 2–3 options at the start of class — a deep-noticing photo, a memory recall game, a slow sentence study. Same underlying skill, but the choice is the point.

Physiological Sigh, Student-Chosen

Double inhale through the nose, long exhale through the mouth, 3–5 times — taught explicitly as a tool students can use on their own, especially when arriving over-aroused from recess or a tense hallway moment.

Attention Endurance Levels, Self-Selected

Let students pick their own duration (60 sec / 2 min / 3 min) for a sustained-looking or slow-sentence task, and self-track over the month as they build stamina.

The 60-Second Brain Dump, Choice of Format

Timer, write every distracting thought, crumple, toss. Let students choose whether to write words, draw, or list — choosing the format is itself a small autonomy win.

Self-monitoring also means noticing feelings and workload before they derail focus — low-disclosure, so recess drama doesn't reopen in front of the class.

Silent Dot Scale

Students place a dot on a 1–5 mood scale as they walk in — no words exchanged, no explanation required. Private self-report as a taught skill, not a demand.

Name It to Tame It Card

One sentence: "Right now I feel ___ because ___." The card goes into a parking-lot envelope on the wall — acknowledged and held, not necessarily resolved this second.

Materials Triage Grid

A 3-box grid: Need Now / Need Today / Need This Week. Students sort their own workload before the period starts — planning as a taught skill rather than an assumption.

Third-Person Self-Talk

Students silently rehearse a challenge using their own name instead of "I" — "You've got this, [Name]." Self-distancing research shows this small shift reduces anxiety and improves follow-through.

The Emotional Battery Check

Students draw a simple battery icon on paper or a index card and fill it to reflect their energy level (0–100%). Below it, they write one "charging factor" (something giving them energy) or one "draining factor" (something taking energy). Acknowledging internal physiological states reduces cognitive friction caused by unaddressed stress.

Weather Report Metaphor

Ask students to write down or draw today's "internal weather report" (e.g., "Sunny with a chance of sudden lightning" or "Heavy morning fog, waiting to clear"). Using figurative language creates psychological distance from overwhelming emotions, shifting brain activity from affective processing to abstract reasoning

Pairs well with Relevance: letting students choose which retrieval question to answer first gives autonomy over a relevance-building task.
